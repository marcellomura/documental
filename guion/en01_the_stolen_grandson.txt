On the afternoon of June 25th, 1978, the loudest sound on Earth was coming from Buenos Aires.

More than seventy thousand people were on their feet inside the Monumental stadium. Argentina had just beaten the Netherlands, three goals to one. For the first time in history, Argentina were champions of the world. Confetti fell like snow. Car horns sang all night long.

The next day, just a few miles away, while the country was still celebrating, a twenty-three-year-old woman went into labor inside the Central Military Hospital.

She was not a patient. She was a prisoner.

She gave birth handcuffed to a stretcher. She held her baby boy for about five hours. She gave him a name. Then he was taken from her, and she never saw him again.

Two months later, she was dead.

Her mother would spend the next thirty-six years searching for that baby. She would become one of the most famous grandmothers on the planet. She would help open a new era in the science of DNA. And the boy she was looking for would grow up to write a song about people like his mother, without the faintest idea that he was singing about himself.

This is the story of Laura, of Estela, and of a grandson who was stolen twice. Once from his mother's arms. And once from his own name.

To understand how a newborn could vanish from a hospital, you have to understand what Argentina was in 1978.

Two years earlier, on March 24th, 1976, the armed forces overthrew the government and seized control of the country. The military junta said it was restoring order. What it actually built was a machine for making people disappear.

Students, workers, union leaders, journalists, anyone suspected of opposing the regime could be pulled from a street, a café, or their own bed in the middle of the night. There was no arrest on record. No charges. No trial. The victims were taken to secret detention centers hidden inside police stations, military bases, even a naval school in the middle of the capital. They were tortured. Most of them were murdered. Their bodies were buried in unmarked graves, or thrown from airplanes into the sea.

Human rights groups estimate that as many as thirty thousand people disappeared. Argentina gave the world a terrible new word: los desaparecidos. The disappeared.

But the regime had a special plan for one kind of prisoner. Pregnant women were often kept alive just long enough to give birth. Then the mothers were killed, and their babies were handed out like spoils of war. To military families. To police officers. To people close to the regime. They were registered as someone else's children, with fake birth certificates signed by complicit doctors.

The logic was chilling. The children of the enemy would be raised by families loyal to the regime, sometimes by the very men who had killed their parents. And they would never, ever know.

Human rights organizations estimate that around five hundred babies were stolen this way.

Laura Estela Carlotto was born in the city of La Plata in 1955. She was the eldest of four children. Her mother, Estela, was a school principal. They were an ordinary middle-class family, and for years, politics was something that happened to other people.

Laura was different. She studied history at the university. She was idealistic, outspoken, and deeply political. Like thousands of young Argentines of her generation, she joined a Peronist youth movement and believed she could change her country.

In 1977, the machine came for the Carlottos.

In August, Laura's father was kidnapped. He was held for twenty-five days and tortured. The family only got him back by paying a ransom of forty million pesos.

Then, in late November, Laura herself was kidnapped in Buenos Aires, together with her partner, Walmir Oscar Montoya. She was twenty-two years old, and two and a half months pregnant.

They ended up in a secret prison on the outskirts of La Plata. The captors had given it a nickname: La Cacha, after a cartoon witch who made people vanish.

Within weeks, Montoya was murdered. His body was buried as an unknown man in a mass grave. It would take thirty-two years for forensic scientists to give him back his name.

Estela did what every family did. She went to police stations, army barracks, churches and courts. She wrote to generals and bishops. And everywhere, she heard the same answer. We have no record of your daughter.

Then, in April 1978, a woman who had just been released from La Cacha brought her a message. Laura was alive. They were feeding her a little better now. The baby was due in June. And if it was a boy, Laura wanted to call him Guido, after her father.

In that moment, Estela understood that she was no longer looking for one person. She was looking for two.

That same month, she joined a small group of women who had begun meeting quietly in Buenos Aires. Their grandchildren had vanished with their parents, or had been born in captivity. They called themselves the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo.

On June 26th, 1978, one day after the World Cup final, Laura gave birth at the Central Military Hospital. She was chained to the stretcher through the entire delivery. She was allowed to hold her son for about five hours. She named him Guido, exactly as she had promised. Then she was sedated, the baby was taken away, and she was driven back to La Cacha. Alone.

Two months later, on August 25th, 1978, the family was summoned to a police station in Isidro Casanova, on the edge of Buenos Aires. The official version was that Laura had been killed in a shootout with security forces at a roadside checkpoint.

It was a lie.

Half of her face had been destroyed by blows. There was a bullet wound in her abdomen, as if someone had wanted to erase any trace of the child she had carried.

There was no baby. No record of a baby. Nothing.

In a cruel way, Estela was one of the lucky ones. Most families of the disappeared never received a body. She was able to bury her daughter in La Plata. And then, instead of collapsing, she retired from her school and turned her grief into a full-time job.

Every Thursday, the mothers and grandmothers walked in slow circles around the Plaza de Mayo, right in front of the presidential palace. They covered their heads with white scarves. The first of those scarves had been cloth diapers. Supporters of the regime called them the madwomen of the Plaza.

But the grandmothers didn't just protest. They became detectives. They collected anonymous tips. They studied birth certificates. They sat outside schools and in playgrounds, looking like any other old ladies, hoping to recognize a nose, a smile, the way a child walked.

In 1983, democracy finally returned to Argentina.

But the grandmothers faced a problem no one had ever solved. Even when they found a child they believed was theirs, how could they prove it? The parents were dead. Paternity tests needed the parents. Blood types were not enough. The only living relatives were the grandparents.

So the grandmothers did something extraordinary. They traveled abroad, knocking on the doors of universities and scientific academies, asking one simple question. Can a grandmother's blood identify her grandchild?

In the United States, they found an answer in an American geneticist named Mary-Claire King. Working with other scientists, she helped develop a statistical method that compared genetic markers across an entire family, grandparents, aunts and uncles, to calculate the probability that a child belonged to it. It became known as the grandparentage index. Its accuracy was ninety-nine point nine nine percent.

In 1984, it was used for the first time to return a stolen girl, Paula Logares, to her real family.

Three years later, Argentina created a National Genetic Data Bank. Families of the disappeared gave their blood, so that even after the grandmothers were gone, their DNA would keep searching.

There was one more question that science could answer for Estela.

In 1985, a team of forensic anthropologists, working with the legendary American scientist Clyde Snow, exhumed Laura's body. They were not there to identify her. They knew exactly who she was. They were there to read her bones.

After examining her pelvis, Snow took Estela aside and told her: Estela, you are a grandmother.

The bones proved it. Laura had carried a full-term pregnancy and given birth. The baby was real. And somewhere out there, he might still be alive.

In 1989, Estela became president of the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo. For the next twenty-five years, she was the face of the search. She met presidents and popes, and received the United Nations Prize in the Field of Human Rights.

And one by one, the grandchildren started coming home. Each of them got a number. Grandchild number one hundred was found in 2009. Estela celebrated every single one of them, as if each one were her own.

But Guido never appeared.

Estela turned seventy. Then eighty. People began to whisper that she would die without ever finding him.

About two hundred miles southwest of Buenos Aires, in the flat farmland near the town of Olavarria, a boy named Ignacio Hurban had grown up on a country estate. His parents, Juana and Clemente, were rural workers who looked after the land of a local landowner. They were humble people, and they loved him.

Ignacio had one passion: music. As a child, he started piano lessons. As a teenager, he went to study at a conservatory near Buenos Aires. He became a jazz pianist, a composer and a teacher. Eventually, he became the director of a music school in Olavarria. He married. He led a jazz orchestra. By every measure, he had a good life.

In 2010, Ignacio took part in an event in Buenos Aires called Music and Memory. There, he heard the story of another stolen grandson who had recovered his identity. That night, before falling asleep, a strange thought crossed his mind, and he said it out loud to his partner. What if my parents aren't my parents?

Then he let it go.

On March 24th, 2014, the Day of Remembrance for the victims of the dictatorship, Ignacio released a song. It was called Para la Memoria. For Memory. He wrote the lyrics, played the accordion, and sang it himself. It was a song about the disappeared.

He had no idea he was singing about his own mother and father.

On June 2nd, 2014, Ignacio turned thirty-six. At least, that's what his documents said.

That night, his wife, Celeste, came home with a question that someone had asked her that same day. Did Ignacio know he was adopted? Did he know that, as a newborn, he had been handed to Juana and Clemente by the landowner they worked for?

Even his birthday was a lie. His real birthday was June 26th. The day after the World Cup final.

The next day, Ignacio went to see the woman who had spoken to Celeste. She told him what she had heard: that he might be the son of political prisoners. That same night, he sat down and wrote an email to the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo.

Nothing pointed to him. He was on nobody's list. He came forward on his own. In July, he gave a blood sample. A few drops of blood, to be compared against the genetic bank the grandmothers had fought so hard to build.

On August 5th, 2014, Estela de Carlotto was at the Grandmothers' headquarters when her phone rang. It was a federal judge, Maria Servini, who had once promised her: I will find your grandson. The judge asked her to come to her office right away. She didn't say why.

At the courthouse, the judge gave her the news. They had found him. Guido. Alive. Thirty-six years old. A musician, living in Olavarria.

Estela let out a cry. Her hands were shaking so badly that she could barely dial her daughter's number. A court employee had to hold her from behind so she wouldn't fall.

That afternoon, in front of a wall of cameras, an eighty-three-year-old woman who had spent half her life looking for one face said the words that made an entire country cry.

I didn't want to die without hugging him.

Headlines around the world told the story. In Argentina, strangers wept in front of their televisions. Grandchild number one hundred and fourteen had come home.

The next day, grandmother and grandson met for the first time. They talked for hours. Estela would later say that with him, her daughter Laura had come back too.

Ignacio chose to keep his first name. Today, he is Ignacio Montoya Carlotto. The courts would later investigate how he had been handed over as a baby. But he refused to hate the couple who raised him. He has said that the idea of Juana and Clemente going to prison because of his story is something he could not bear.

Because this is the part of the story that has no easy answer. Can you love the people who raised you on a lie? Can the truth hurt and heal at the same time? For Ignacio, both things are true.

His story is not just an Argentine story. It asks the oldest human question there is. Who am I, really?

The grandmothers' fight changed the world far beyond Argentina. Genetic identification like theirs is now used to reunite families torn apart by war, dictatorship and trafficking. And when the United Nations wrote the Convention on the Rights of the Child, it included every child's right to an identity, a name, and a family. In Argentina, those clauses are still called the Argentine articles.

Today, the grandmothers have found one hundred and forty grandchildren. Hundreds more are still missing.

They are now in their forties and fifties. They have jobs, partners, children of their own. Some of them might be watching this video right now.

If you were born in Argentina between 1975 and 1983, and you have ever had even the smallest doubt about where you came from, the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo are still waiting for your call.

Many of the grandmothers have already died without ever hugging the children they searched for. But their blood is still in that genetic bank. Still searching. Still waiting.

In 1978, a dictatorship tried to erase a baby's name. It failed. Because a mother chose that name before he was born. Because a prisoner smuggled it out of a secret camp. And because a grandmother refused, for thirty-six years, to let the world forget it.

His name was Guido.

And in the end, he came home singing.
